*A short way to start talking with your teen about who they really are — before dinner interrupts it again.*

There's a conversation most parents of teenagers carry around for months without ever having it. Not a crisis conversation. Not a discipline conversation. Just the quiet one — how are you actually doing, underneath the shrug and the one-word answers, and do you know how much I see in you.

You've probably tried to start it. And then dinner happened. Or homework. Or the moment passed because it didn't feel like the right one, and you genuinely weren't sure what you'd say next.

That's not a failure of love. It's a failure of a starting line.

## Why does this conversation keep stalling out?

Most parents don't avoid this conversation because they don't care. They avoid it because every attempt they've made has followed the same shape — and that shape doesn't work for this particular conversation.

It usually opens with a concern ("I've noticed you've been..."), which tells a teenager this is about a problem, not about them. It's framed as something they have to sit through, which makes them the audience rather than a participant. There's an expected right answer, which makes silence the safest option. And there's an unspoken assumption that it needs to get resolved in one sitting — so if it gets uncomfortable, the parent quietly marks it as a failed attempt and doesn't try again for months.

None of that is a parenting failure. It's just a script built for a different kind of conversation, borrowed for one it was never designed to hold.

## A different way in

A few years ago, at my own kitchen table, I tried something different with my own kids — not a script I'd read somewhere, but four moves I'd worked out from watching what actually opened people up versus what shut them down.

**I owned my part first.** Before I asked anything of them, I said something true about myself as a parent — not a confession session, just one honest sentence: "I know as parents we've made mistakes, and you probably haven't always felt accepted and understood." That sentence does something specific. It lowers a teenager's guard before you've asked them to lower theirs. You go first.

**I said the thing the whole conversation was for.** Not implied — said, out loud: "You are valuable, and what you bring to our family and to the world around you is valuable." If the entire point of the conversation is that your teen would hear that clearly, don't bury it. Lead with it.

**I asked instead of telling.** One open question, with a second half that matters as much as the first: "What feels true about how I see you right now? Was there anything that didn't feel true?" That second half tells your teen you're not the final word on who they are — they are. That's what makes it safe to answer honestly instead of performing the answer they think you want.

**I gave permission before anyone needed it.** Out loud, to the whole room, before the silence got uncomfortable: "You don't have to share tonight. It's yours. You get to disagree, take time, or feel awkward about this — most families never have this conversation at all, so we're all learning together."

That last line does more work than anything else in the sequence. It removes the pressure that kills this conversation before it starts — the fear that there's a wrong answer, or a deadline on being ready.

## If it gets hard

Sometimes it does. Someone goes quiet, pushes back, or says "this isn't me." That's not a sign it's going wrong. For most families, this is the first time this particular conversation has ever been attempted at all.

When that happens, it helps to say something like: "This is more of a reference than a rulebook. This isn't a diagnosis — it's meant to help us talk. You get to disagree, need time, or feel awkward. Most families never have this conversation, so we're all learning together."

And if someone leaves the table after that — let them. Come back in a week, one-to-one, without an audience. The goal was never to finish the conversation in one sitting. It was to start it. A conversation that ends in silence tonight and gets picked back up gently next week isn't a failed attempt — it's the same conversation, still open.

I close it the same way every time, regardless of how far the conversation got: "I think our family is beginning to find out — together."

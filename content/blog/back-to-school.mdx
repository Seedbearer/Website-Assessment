Every September brings the same ritual. The new pencil case. The fresh notebooks with their unmarked pages. The uniform that still fits, or the one you've had to buy a size up for. The quiet hope that this year might be different — that something about the clean slate of a new school year might reset whatever was hard about the last one.

And underneath all of it, often unspoken: a child who is walking back into an environment that will spend the next ten months telling them who they are.

Not deliberately. Not cruelly. But schools are powerful identity-shapers — through comparison, through grading, through peer pressure, through the implicit hierarchies of who is good at what and who isn't. Your child will spend hundreds of hours this year in a system that measures them. And how they make sense of those measurements will be shaped almost entirely by whether they arrived with a secure sense of who they are that exists independently of how well they perform.

None of this is inherently wrong. School is supposed to challenge and stretch and expose children to a wider world. The problem comes when a child arrives without an anchor — without something inside them that holds steady when the measurements come back low, or the belonging proves conditional, or the peer narrative is unkind.

## What the anchor actually is

Gordon Neufeld, the developmental psychologist, argues that a child who is securely attached to their parents can withstand the pressures of the peer environment without being defined by them. The attachment to parents is the anchor that holds the child steady while the storm of school happens around them.

But attachment is not just presence. It is attunement. It is the child knowing — in their body, not just their head — that there is someone who sees them. Not their grades. Not their behaviour. Not their performance. *Them.* The specific, particular, unrepeatable person they are.

Proverbs 22:6 says to train up a child in the way *they* should go — not the way you hoped, not the way the school thinks they should go, but the way that is native to their particular design. Before school can tell your child who they are, you have the opportunity to tell them something truer and more specific: here is who I see when I look at you. Here is what I notice in you. Here is the thing in you that the world has never seen before in exactly this form.

## Three conversations worth having before the first day

## What to watch for in the first few weeks

The first weeks of a school year are identity-forming. Pay attention during this time — not anxiously, but with genuine curiosity. What you're watching for is not performance but orientation: where is your child finding their sense of worth right now?

- Do they come home talking about what they *did* — or who they *were* in the doing of it? A child who says "I helped someone today" is orienting around identity. A child who only reports grades is orienting around performance.
- When something goes badly, how do they interpret it? "I got it wrong" is recoverable. "I am stupid" is identity-level. The second one needs more than comfort — it needs a conversation about who they actually are.
- Are they pulling away or staying connected? Neufeld's research is clear: a child who starts preferring peers over parents in times of difficulty is a child whose attachment anchor is shifting. This is not punishment for bad parenting — it is information about what the child needs.
- What do they say about themselves when they're tired and their guard is down? The things children say about themselves when they're not performing reveal the operating system running underneath.

## The most important school supply

The pencil case is sorted. The uniform fits. The bus route is confirmed.

The most important thing your child takes to school this year is a settled sense of who they are that doesn't depend on how the day goes. That isn't built in the week before September. It's built in the ordinary moments across the whole year — the way you greet them when they get home, the questions you ask at dinner, the way you respond when things go wrong, the specific things you notice and name about who they are.

But if you haven't built it yet — the first day of school is as good a day as any to start. You don't need a programme. You need five minutes tonight, a specific observation about who your child is, and the willingness to say it out loud.

That's the anchor. Everything else is logistics.
